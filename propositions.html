Step 1

Our Standard Evaluation Framework

Every proposition is rated the same way, on five factors that can be checked against official documents and cited sources. Each card also describes, without a rating, who could gain, who pays or gives something up, and how hard the measure would be to change later. The standard weights are already set, so you can go straight to Step 2.

Factors and standard weights

    How these ratings behave

    Most measures land between 2 and 4 overall. That is expected. A measure can be strong on one factor and weak on another, and averaging pulls the result toward the middle. So each card leads with what the measure is strong on and weak on. Read that first. The overall number comes second. It is shown out of 5 and as a percentage of the top rating, so 2.5 of 5 is 50%, the 3.0 midpoint is 60% and 5 of 5 is 100%.

    How scoring works
    1. Choose what matters to you. Set how important each of the five factors is. Your weights become percentages of your total weighting. With the standard weights, Evidence strength is .
    2. Review the evidence ratings. Each proposition has a rating from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) on each factor, with a written reason and sources.
    3. Calculate the weighted result. Each factor adds points in proportion to its percentage and its rating. A factor rated 5 of 5 adds its full percentage; one rated 3 of 5 adds three fifths of it.
    4. Understand what it means. The result summarizes this tool's recorded ratings under your priorities. It is not a chance of success, a share of voters, a guarantee of benefit, or a recommendation to vote Yes or No.
    Show calculation

    Each card's calculation table has three columns. Importance is how much each factor counts for you. Rating is how the measure scores on it. Points are what the factor adds to the result.

    Weight share of a factor = its weight ÷ the total of all five weights × 100.

    Points from a factor = its weight ÷ the total weight of the factors that are weighted and rated × its rating ÷ 5 × 100.

    Weighted evidence rating = the sum of those points. The tool rounds the average rating to one decimal (for example 3.19 becomes 3.2) and uses that rounded value for the score, the quality word and your lean, so the percentage is shown from it (3.2 of 5 = 64%). Displayed numbers may not add up exactly because of rounding.

    A factor set to Ignore (weight 0) is left out. A factor that is Not rated is left out too, and the other weights are scaled up to fill its place. Missing evidence is never counted as 0 or as 5. If every weight is 0, no evidence score is calculated.

    How this tool works

    How your answer and the evidence are shown, where the facts come from, when they were checked, and your privacy.

    How your answer and the evidence are shown

    1. Your answer is your result. Each card and the list in Step 3 show Yes, No or Not sure exactly as you chose it. The tool never changes or overrides it. A card you leave blank shows Not answered, which is different from Not sure.
    2. The evidence check is a weighted average of this tool's ratings, counting each factor by its weight (the standard weights unless you customize them). Factors set to Ignore, and any rating marked Not rated, are left out. It runs from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong), shown to one decimal place. It is shown the same way whatever you answer. If every factor is set to Ignore, or none of your weighted factors is rated, no score is shown.
    3. Strong on and weak on list the factors you weigh that rate 4 or higher, and 2 or lower. Everything between is in the middle. This is shown before the overall number, because one average can hide a measure that is very strong on one thing and weak on another.
    4. Who could gain, who pays and how hard it is to change later are described, not rated. They come from the official analysis, the fiscal estimates and each measure's legal text. Whether a gain is worth its cost is your call.
    5. The 3.0 midpoint is the middle of the 1 to 5 scale, marked on each bar. It is a reference point, not a pass mark. It is not a probability that a measure will work, and it has not been statistically validated.
    6. When your answer and the evidence point different ways, for example Yes on a measure rated below 3.0, it is a reason to read the other side's argument again, not a sign that you are wrong. Some people vote yes anyway because the goal matters most. Others vote no and wait for a better-written version. Each card links to the argument on the other side of your answer.

    Sources and methodology

    How the weights were set. The criteria and weights reflect the framework's design choices about what matters when evaluating a ballot proposition. They provide a consistent starting point; they are not a scientifically proven or universally agreed-upon set of ideal weights. The choice of weights can influence the results, and reasonable people may disagree about the relative importance of different factors.

    The purpose is to make the evaluation process more structured and transparent, not to present a numerical score as an objective verdict or a recommendation on how to vote. Voters are encouraged to review the evidence, examine the assumptions, and make their own decisions.

    What the framework cannot establish. It cannot predict whether a measure will succeed, and it cannot weigh your values for you. Ratings are analytical judgments based on the cited sources, and where information is thin a rating is marked as limited or Not rated rather than scored low.

    When this was checked. Facts were checked against official sources on October 22 and 23, 2026, the YES and NO meanings against the official voter guide on October 26, source links on October 24, and wording for neutrality on October 26. These checks were automated. The ratings are this project's own and are not reviewed by any outside organization. Confirm your ballot with your county elections office.

    Facts. Each card's facts come from the Secretary of State's official voter guide (titles, what YES and NO mean, official arguments and legal text) and the Legislative Analyst's Office (fiscal estimates). Independent reporting and research are used where official sources are silent, and are labeled as Independent. Sources with a stake in a measure are labeled by that interest, such as Industry association, Advocacy group or Interested party. Political party positions are not listed, because they could not be sourced the same way for every measure. Each card names the biggest risk if the measure passes. What a NO vote keeps is in "A NO vote means". Campaign claims are attributed to the side that makes them. Every card lists its sources, numbered so each rating can point to them.

    PDF sources. PDF links open in your browser's PDF viewer, which can stay blank for a few seconds while a long document loads. If a PDF does not appear, use the web page version where one is listed, or your browser's download button. Some in-app browsers cannot show PDFs. Open the page in Safari or Chrome instead.

    Ratings. The ratings are structured analytical judgments made for this tool, drafted with AI research assistance and published by the tool's creator. They are not official government ratings or scientific measurements. One rubric is applied to all 14 measures, whatever the topic. Striped dots mean a rating rests on limited information, a half-filled dot marks a half point, and Not rated means there was too little verified information to score it. Missing information is not treated as a good or bad sign.

    A 2 or 4 falls between the descriptions. For Low risk of side effects, 2 means a specific risk named in the official analysis, the legal text or cited reporting, such as clinic closures, rejected ballots or lawsuits. Accountability and results is the average of its two parts, so it can be a half point, such as 3.5. For checks on the work, 2 means a self-executing rule or limited reporting with no review, and 4 means independent audits plus public reporting. For Cost clarity, 2 means the official analysis gives a direction but no dollars. For measurable results, 4 means a clear observable rule or required reporting on results.

    Your privacy. Your answers and weights are saved only in this browser's local storage so they are there when you return. They are never sent anywhere, and the tool asks for no name or contact details. Clear my answers removes saved answers. Weights stay until you change them or pick Standard. The page loads fonts from Google Fonts and a visitor counter from GoatCounter, and like any web request these send your IP address and browser details to those services. GoatCounter records one visit each time this page loads, using only the page's fixed name and title, plus the referring site, browser, screen size, language and country. It also records two anonymous counts, when someone answers at least one question and when someone answers all 14. It is never sent which propositions you open or how you answer. Its privacy policy says it does not store IP addresses or set cookies. This page keeps a small note in local storage so each count is sent only once. If storage is blocked, each count is sent at most once per page load. Source links take you to other websites only when you click them.
