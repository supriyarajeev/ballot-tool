Step 1

Our Standard Evaluation Framework

To make it easier for voters to compare California's ballot propositions, this tool uses one consistent evaluation framework across all 14 propositions.

The framework considers factors such as public benefit, strength of evidence, safety, accountability, feasibility, and fiscal impact. Each factor is assessed using available evidence and supporting sources, with ratings and explanations provided for voters to examine.

The framework also uses default weights so voters can explore the propositions without having to configure a scoring model themselves. The standard weights are already set, so you can go straight to Step 2.

Factors and standard weights

    Each measure's evidence score is the weighted average of its ratings on these factors, from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). A factor with weight 5 counts two and a half times as much as one with weight 2. With the standard weights, Net benefit and Evidence strength together make up 10 of the 24 weight points.

    How scoring works

    1. Choose what matters to you. Set how important each of the seven factors is. Your weights become percentages of your total weighting. With the standard weights, Net benefit is .
    2. Review the evidence ratings. Each proposition has a rating from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) on each factor, with a written reason and sources.
    3. Calculate the weighted result. Each factor adds points in proportion to its percentage and its rating. A factor rated 5 of 5 adds its full percentage; one rated 3 of 5 adds three fifths of it.
    4. Understand what it means. The result summarizes this tool's recorded ratings under your priorities. It is not a chance of success, a share of voters, a guarantee of benefit, or a recommendation to vote Yes or No.
    Show calculation

    Weight share of a factor = its weight ÷ the total of all seven weights × 100.

    Points from a factor = its weight ÷ the total weight of the factors that are weighted and rated × its rating ÷ 5 × 100.

    Weighted evidence rating = the sum of those points. The tool rounds the average rating to one decimal (for example 3.19 becomes 3.2) and uses that rounded value for the score, the quality word and your lean, so the percentage is shown from it (3.2 of 5 = 64%). Displayed numbers may not add up exactly because of rounding.

    A factor set to Ignore (weight 0) is left out. A factor that is Not rated is left out too, and the other weights are scaled up to fill its place. Missing evidence is never counted as 0 or as 5. If every weight is 0, no evidence score is calculated.

    How were the weights determined?

    The criteria and weights reflect the framework's design choices about what matters when evaluating a ballot proposition. They provide a consistent starting point; they are not a scientifically proven or universally agreed-upon set of ideal weights. The choice of weights can influence the results, and reasonable people may disagree about the relative importance of different factors.

    The purpose is to make the evaluation process more structured and transparent, not to present a numerical score as an objective verdict or a recommendation on how to vote. Voters are encouraged to review the evidence, examine the assumptions, and make their own decisions.

    What the framework cannot establish. It cannot predict whether a measure will succeed, and it cannot weigh your values for you. Ratings are analytical judgments based on the cited sources, and where information is thin a rating is marked as limited or Not rated rather than scored low.

    How your answer and the evidence are shown

    1. Your answer is your result. Each card and the list in Step 3 show Yes, No or Not sure exactly as you chose it. The tool never changes or overrides it. A card you leave blank shows Not answered, which is different from Not sure.
    2. The evidence check is a weighted average of this tool's ratings, counting each factor by its weight (the standard weights unless you customize them). Factors set to Ignore, and any rating marked Not rated, are left out. It runs from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong), shown to one decimal place. It is shown the same way whatever you answer. If every factor is set to Ignore, or none of your weighted factors is rated, no score is shown.
    3. The 3.0 midpoint is the middle of the 1 to 5 scale, marked on each bar. It is a reference point, not a pass mark. It is not a probability that a measure will work, and it has not been statistically validated.
    4. When your answer and the evidence point different ways, for example Yes on a measure rated below 3.0, it is a reason to read the other side's argument again, not a sign that you are wrong. Some people vote yes anyway because the goal matters most. Others vote no and wait for a better-written version. Each card links to the argument on the other side of your answer.

    Sources and methodology

    Facts. Each card's facts come from the Secretary of State's official voter guide (titles, what YES and NO mean, official arguments and legal text) and the Legislative Analyst's Office (fiscal estimates). Independent reporting and research are used where official sources are silent, and are labeled as Independent. Campaign claims are attributed to the side that makes them. Every card lists its sources, numbered so each rating can point to them.

    Ratings. The ratings are structured analytical judgments made for this tool, drafted with AI research assistance and published by the tool's creator. They are not official government ratings or scientific measurements. One rubric is applied to all 14 measures, whatever the topic. Dashed dots mean a rating rests on limited information, and Not rated means there was too little verified information to score it. Missing information is not treated as a good or bad sign.

    FeasibilityNew, untested systems with many undefined termsExisting agencies, but tight timelines or open questionsExisting systems with little new work
    Fiscal clarityNo official estimate and unclear directionOfficial dollar range or bound, key costs unknownClear official yearly and total figures
    Outcome clarityNo measurable result definedSpending or rules are measurable, but real-world results are not trackedSpecific numeric result written into law and reported

    A 2 or 4 falls between the descriptions. For Accountability, 2 means a self-executing rule or limited reporting with no review, and 4 means independent audits plus public reporting. For Fiscal clarity, 2 means the official analysis gives a direction but no dollars. For Outcome clarity, 4 means a clear observable rule or required reporting on results.
