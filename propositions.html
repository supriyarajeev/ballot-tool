Step 1

Our Standard Evaluation Framework

To make it easier for voters to compare California's ballot propositions, this tool uses one consistent evaluation framework across all 14 propositions.

The framework considers factors such as public benefit, strength of evidence, safety, accountability, feasibility, and fiscal impact. Each factor is assessed using available evidence and supporting sources, with ratings and explanations provided for voters to examine.

The framework also uses default weights so voters can explore the propositions without having to configure a scoring model themselves. The standard weights are already set, so you can go straight to Step 2.

Factors and standard weights

    Each measure's evidence score is the weighted average of its ratings on these factors, from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). A factor with weight 5 counts two and a half times as much as one with weight 2. With the standard weights, Net benefit and Evidence strength together make up 10 of the 24 weight points.

    How were the weights determined?

    The criteria and weights reflect the framework's design choices about what matters when evaluating a ballot proposition. They provide a consistent starting point; they are not a scientifically proven or universally agreed-upon set of ideal weights. The choice of weights can influence the results, and reasonable people may disagree about the relative importance of different factors.

    The purpose is to make the evaluation process more structured and transparent, not to present a numerical score as an objective verdict or a recommendation on how to vote. Voters are encouraged to review the evidence, examine the assumptions, and make their own decisions.

    What the framework cannot establish. It cannot predict whether a measure will succeed, and it cannot weigh your values for you. Ratings are analytical judgments based on the cited sources, and where information is thin a rating is marked as limited or Not rated rather than scored low.

    How your result is worked out

    1. Your preference is how you feel about the measure's goal. Against or Strongly against always shows Leans toward NO. That reflects your view, not a finding that the measure is bad. Not sure and Not answered never count as support or opposition.
    2. The evidence score is a weighted average of this tool's ratings, counting each factor by its weight (the standard weights unless you customize them). Factors set to Ignore, and any rating marked Not rated, are left out. It runs from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong), shown to one decimal place. If every factor is set to Ignore, it shows Evidence not weighted. If you weigh factors but none of them has a rating for a measure, it shows No evidence score available. Either way the result follows your preference alone.
    3. When you support the goal, the evidence score decides whether the evidence backs you up, using the table below. Either way, the vote is yours.

    About the cutoffs. The 2.0 and 3.0 lines are illustrative rules of thumb. They are not probabilities that a measure will work, and they have not been statistically validated. Mixed / uncertain means you like the goal but the measure rates below the line on what you said matters. Some people vote yes anyway because the goal matters most. Others vote no and wait for a better-written version.

    Sources and methodology

    Facts. Each card's facts come from the Secretary of State's official voter guide (titles, what YES and NO mean, official arguments and legal text) and the Legislative Analyst's Office (fiscal estimates). Independent reporting and research are used where official sources are silent, and are labeled as Independent. Campaign claims are attributed to the side that makes them. Every card lists its sources, numbered so each rating can point to them.

    Ratings. The ratings are structured analytical judgments made for this tool, drafted with AI research assistance and published by the tool's creator. They are not official government ratings or scientific measurements. One rubric is applied to all 14 measures, whatever the topic. Dashed dots mean a rating rests on limited information, and Not rated means there was too little verified information to score it. Missing information is not treated as a good or bad sign.

    A 2 or 4 falls between the descriptions. For Accountability, 2 means a self-executing rule or limited reporting with no review, and 4 means independent audits plus public reporting. For Fiscal clarity, 2 means the official analysis gives a direction but no dollars. For Outcome clarity, 4 means a clear observable rule or required reporting on results.
